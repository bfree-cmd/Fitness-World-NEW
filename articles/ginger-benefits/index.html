What Is Ginger?

Ginger comes from the rhizome of Zingiber officinale. It is widely used in cooking and has a long history of traditional use.

Fresh ginger, dried ginger, powdered ginger and concentrated supplements are not identical. Research on supplements should not automatically be assumed to apply to a cup of ginger tea or food made with ginger.

What Does Research Say About Ginger and Nausea?

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) reports that ginger has been studied for several types of nausea and vomiting. Evidence is strongest for pregnancy-related nausea, while results are less certain for other situations.

That distinction matters: ginger should not be described as a universal treatment for nausea, and persistent or severe symptoms deserve appropriate medical evaluation.

Simple Ways to Use Ginger

  • Steep thin slices of fresh ginger in hot water.
  • Grate a small amount into stir-fries, soups or marinades.
  • Add fresh or powdered ginger to foods for flavor.

Food-level use is different from taking a concentrated supplement. If you are considering a supplement, especially alongside medication, check with a qualified health professional.

Safety and Side Effects

NCCIH notes that ginger can cause mild side effects such as abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea or gas in some people. Herbal products can also interact with medicines.

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, gallbladder disease, medication use and other personal medical factors are reasons to ask a qualified clinician or pharmacist before using concentrated ginger products.

BOTTOM LINE

Ginger can be a useful culinary ingredient and may help some forms of nausea, but the evidence is specific rather than universal. Use realistic expectations, separate food use from supplement use, and prioritize safety.
